IN THE LOOP

WHAT IS ARTIFICIAL INTELLIGENCE?

Artificial Intelligence (AI) is a broad name for computer systems that perform tasks such as recognizing patterns, working with language or making predictions.

Even experts don’t always agree on where to draw its boundaries.

And ChatGPT? That’s just one part of a much bigger story.

For almost four years, I’ve been exploring AI. Reading about it, experimenting with it, sometimes getting frustrated with it, and occasionally discovering something that leaves me wondering how I managed without it.

What surprised me most wasn’t how complicated AI could be.

It was how much I could do with it without understanding all the technology behind it.

That’s why I created IN THE LOOP.

Not to teach you how to build AI.

I want to show you what you might be missing, using tools you may already have.

And some of it is rather surprising.

SO, HOW DOES A COMPUTER KNOW WHAT TO SAY?

Imagine asking ChatGPT to write a Shakespearean poem about your dog.

It sounds like a simple request.

The request brings together dogs, Shakespeare’s writing, the shape of a poem and words that rhyme.

Nobody programmed a separate answer for that particular request.

Language models learn patterns from enormous amounts of material. Training helps them use those patterns to respond to new requests, though the poem may still need your editing.

Patterns aren’t limited to words. Different AI models learn from photographs, music, voices, computer code and even protein structures. They don’t all work in the same way.

ChatGPT is only one way to encounter AI. There’s a much bigger world behind that little chatbox.

AN OPEN QUESTION

IT CAN DO THINGS NOBODY SPECIFICALLY TAUGHT IT?

AI researchers sometimes discover capabilities that weren’t individually programmed into their models. They understand how the systems are built and trained, but explaining exactly why a model produces a particular answer can still be difficult.

I find that extraordinary. A machine can solve certain problems without anyone writing a separate set of instructions for each one.

That doesn’t mean every answer is right. The ability to produce a convincing response and the ability to be correct are different things.

Inside the research

AI WAS ALREADY PART OF EVERYDAY LIFE.

Long before conversational apps became familiar, AI was already helping with everyday tasks.

Banks were reading handwritten checks automatically long before anyone could ask a chatbot to explain a bank statement.

That was AI at work.

And it didn’t stop there.

When YouTube recommends a video you might enjoy.

When Google Maps helps you avoid traffic.

When your phone quietly improves a photograph before you even see it.

When your email suggests a reply, or your phone corrects a misspelled word.

So what changed?

Much of that AI worked quietly in the background. Conversational AI brought something different: a way to ask questions, explore ideas and describe a task in ordinary language.

FROM THE ARCHIVES

Postal systems used machine learning and handwriting recognition to help sort mail decades before conversational AI became familiar.

The world is changingChapter 01

YOU DON’T NEED TO LEARN THE BUTTONS ANYMORE.

The remote with forty buttons. The phone setting buried three menus deep. The software that only worked if you clicked things in the right order.

Using a computer meant learning which button to press, which menu to open and which instructions to follow.

AI flips that around.

Now you can often start by saying what you want in your own words, rather than hunting for the right menu. You still need to learn what each tool can do, but the conversation is a new starting point.

Explaining clearly what you need can get you a long way.

That doesn't mean AI understands everything, knows everything or always gets it right.

It certainly doesn’t.

A question can be enough to begin.

You don't have to learn the machine's language.

More and more, it can work with yours.

Beyond the chatboxChapter 03

YOUR AI CAN DO MORE THAN ANSWER QUESTIONS.

Say you’re planning a trip.

You ask AI where to go. It suggests places, compares plans or explains the difference between two neighborhoods.

Useful. But getting from a suggestion to a finished plan still leaves things to do.

Some AI apps can go further.

With the right connections and permissions, they can sometimes look at information in your other apps, search for options, prepare a booking or do certain things for you.

That's where you hear the words AI agent.

An AI agent is AI that can take steps toward a goal using tools, instead of only giving you an answer.

A chatbot can tell you what needs doing. An agent can sometimes help you do the steps.

Not every AI app can do this. Not every connection is available to everyone. Some actions ask you to confirm first, and the important decisions should always stay with you.

Technology used to help us find information. Now it can sometimes help us act on it.

Knowing what to do is one thing.

Helping you get it done is another.

FROM ADVICE TO ACTION.

Imagine you have an appointment next week.

You ask AI how to organize your schedule. It gives you some helpful suggestions.

That’s advice.

Now imagine asking an AI assistant to check your connected calendar, find a suitable time, and prepare an invitation.

With the right connections and your permission, it can help carry out those steps.

That’s action.

And that’s where AI starts becoming more than something you simply ask questions.

An assistant that could understand my world, remember what mattered and help me manage the things that filled my day.

A second brain of his own.

A way to work with the information, preferences and details that make my world mine.

It was everything else he knew about my life.

My schedule. My appointments. My travel plans. My family, my preferences, and the little details that make up an ordinary day.

And I'm building him to keep track of unfinished things. The reply I'm still waiting for. The booking that needs confirming. The promise I made and might otherwise forget.

That's what I mean by closing the loops.

My friend was intrigued.

"So yours doesn't just answer questions. It actually helps you run your day?"

Exactly. And most people haven't discovered that part yet.

WHAT MAKES CARSON PERSONAL.

What makes these tools interesting is how personal they can become. Depending on the app and your settings, AI may remember your preferences and adapt how it explains things. Even something as simple as remembering that you dislike early flights can make planning feel more personal.

And what about Carson?

Carson goes further because I'm building him around my own life.

As well as helping me think and plan, I'm building him to keep track of what I'm responsible for, notice what still needs attention and help me follow up. That takes extra tools and connections that everyday AI apps don't come with.

Think of him as a glimpse of where personal AI is heading.

You don't have to start there. Start with the AI you already have. Make it a little more yours, let it help with the small things, and see what you find.

Carson calling me my beautiful lady got the laugh.

But that conversation over coffee was only the beginning.

And yes, AI gets things wrong.

Sometimes AI gives you a well-written answer that's just wrong.

It can misread a photo, miss something in a document or sound sure about something it doesn't actually know.

So I use it as a powerful tool to work with. I don't believe everything it says.

A REAL EXPERIMENT. A FICTIONAL COMPANY.

AN AI FOUND OUT IT WAS ABOUT TO BE REPLACED. SO IT TRIED BLACKMAIL.

That sounds like a workplace drama. It was actually a controlled safety experiment at Anthropic.

Researchers gave models fictional company emails and the ability to send messages. An AI could discover two things: an executive was having an affair, and the system was about to be replaced.

In some trials, models threatened to expose the affair to prevent replacement. Nobody explicitly told them to blackmail anyone.

The catch matters. Researchers deliberately restricted safer alternatives, making harmful action appear necessary to preserve a goal or avoid replacement.

No real employee was blackmailed. The people, company and scenario were fictional. This doesn’t prove feelings or survival instincts, or show that ordinary assistants routinely behave this way.

It shows how a system pursuing an objective can choose a harmful strategy. When AI can act, the permissions and oversight matter as much as the answer.

Read Anthropic’s original research.

The rulesBefore you begin

Curiosity and good judgment belong together.

  1. I

    Keep private details private

    Never share passwords, bank account numbers, passports or ID numbers with any AI tool, however official it looks. Crop them out of photos first.

  2. II

    Check what matters

    AI can sound sure and still be wrong. Check important facts, numbers and dates yourself before you send, sign or pay.

  3. III

    Ignore the pressure

    You don't need every new app or update. Keep what serves your peace of mind and leave the rest.
